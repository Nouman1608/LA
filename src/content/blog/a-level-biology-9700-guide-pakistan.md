A Level Biology is one of the most commonly chosen sciences among Pakistani students heading toward medicine, pharmacy, biotechnology and related degrees — and it's also one of the subjects where students underestimate how different the jump from IGCSE actually is. With the Oct/Nov 2026 series approaching, this guide covers what Cambridge A Level Biology (9700) involves, how it's assessed, and what tends to separate a strong result from an average one.

## What is A Level Biology (9700)?

Cambridge International A Level Biology, syllabus code **9700**, is designed to be studied over two years, split into an AS Level (first year) and the full A Level (second year, building on AS content). Students can also choose to complete just the AS Level as a standalone qualification.

It's a content-heavy subject that combines detailed factual knowledge — cell structure, biochemistry, genetics, physiology — with practical and data-handling skills that are examined directly, not just assumed.

## Exam structure: AS and A Level papers

Cambridge structures 9700 assessment differently at AS and full A Level stage.

**At AS Level**, students typically sit a multiple-choice paper covering AS content, a structured-questions paper testing AS theory, and a paper assessing practical skills such as planning, recording and evaluating experimental work.

**At full A Level**, students additionally sit papers covering the more advanced content — including extended structured and essay-style questions — plus a further practical-skills paper that builds on the AS component with more advanced data analysis and evaluation.

Exact paper numbers and formats are set by Cambridge and reviewed periodically, so it's worth confirming the current combination for your session with your teacher rather than assuming last year's structure carries over unchanged.

## Core topics covered

A Level Biology builds on IGCSE and O Level Biology but goes considerably deeper.

**Cell biology and biochemistry** covers cell structure and ultrastructure, biological molecules — carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids — and enzyme action.

**Genetics and inheritance** covers DNA structure and replication, protein synthesis, inheritance patterns, gene technology, and selection and evolution.

**Physiology** covers transport in plants and animals, gas exchange, and — at full A Level — coordination and response (the nervous and hormonal systems), homeostasis, and reproduction.

**Ecology and the environment** covers energy flow, nutrient cycles, and human impact on ecosystems.

**Microorganisms and immunity** covers infectious disease, immune response, and the biology behind vaccination — a section many students in Pakistan find genuinely useful beyond the exam itself.

Genetics and physiology tend to carry the heaviest, most application-based questions, so they're usually worth extra revision time compared to more descriptive topics like ecology.

## AS Level vs full A Level: understanding the split

Many students in Pakistan take AS Biology in Grade 11 and complete the full A Level across Grade 12, which lines up with how most schools and tuition programmes structure the two years. AS grades and full A Level grades are reported and used differently by universities, so if you're planning applications that require a full A Level, make sure your school has you entered correctly for the second year rather than just carrying forward an AS result.

## Common mistakes students make

A few patterns show up repeatedly among students who underperform relative to their mock results.

**Confusing similar processes.** Osmosis vs diffusion, mitosis vs meiosis, and translocation vs transpiration are frequently mixed up under exam pressure — these are worth drilling specifically with comparison tables rather than separate notes.

**Vague terminology.** Cambridge examiners mark Biology on precise language. Writing "it moves down the concentration gradient" instead of naming the correct process by name can cost marks even when the underlying understanding is correct.

**Leaving practical papers until the last week.** Because they feel less like "content" to revise, practical and data-handling papers often get squeezed out of a revision plan entirely, even though they're a predictable source of marks when practised properly.

## How to prepare for the Oct/Nov 2026 session

Don't just memorise definitions — practise applying them, since A Level Biology examiners frequently reward application over recall, making past-paper structured questions more valuable revision time than re-reading notes. Treat the practical papers seriously, since they test specific skills like identifying variables, describing limitations and interpreting graphs that need deliberate practice, not general subject knowledge. Build topic-linked revision rather than purely chronological revision — genetics links to evolution, transport links to gas exchange — since Biology topics connect heavily and revising by theme close to the exam often helps more than working strictly syllabus-order. And use official mark schemes to learn examiner phrasing, since Biology answers are marked on specific keywords and logic chains, and getting familiar with that language early avoids losing marks on otherwise correct answers.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is A Level Biology a big jump from IGCSE or O Level Biology?** Yes — the content is significantly deeper, especially in genetics and physiology, and exam questions rely much more on application than recall. Students who found IGCSE or O Level Biology mostly about memorisation often need to adjust their approach early.

**Do I need A Level Biology for MDCAT or medical college admission in Pakistan?** Much of the core content overlaps with what's tested at the university admissions stage, but entry test requirements and equivalence rules change, so check current guidance from IBCC and the relevant university or medical college directly rather than assuming A Level content alone is sufficient preparation.

**Can I take A Level Biology without having studied it at IGCSE or O Level?** It's possible but demanding, since the syllabus assumes a working foundation in basic biological concepts. Extra catch-up support in the first term is usually worth arranging in that case.
