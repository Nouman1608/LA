The Cambridge October/November 2026 series opens on **28 September** and runs through to **13 November 2026**, with A Level results due **7 January 2027** and IGCSE/O Level results due **14 January 2027**. If you registered as a private candidate before the 3 September deadline, or your school entered you for this series, you're now inside the most important stretch of the year: the final weeks before the exam hall.

Most students don't lose marks because they didn't study — they lose marks because they studied the wrong things, in the wrong order, too close to the exam. This post gives you a practical, week-by-week structure to fix that, whether you have eight weeks left or three.

## Why This Revision Window Matters

Depending on your subject combination, your first paper could fall anywhere between late September and mid-November. That's a real advantage if you use it well — six to eight weeks is enough time to close most grade gaps — but it disappears fast if revision starts as unstructured re-reading of notes. A timetable forces you to decide, in advance, what gets your time and what doesn't.

It also matters because this session includes a large number of private candidates and retake students alongside first-time school candidates. If you're retaking a paper, your revision plan should look different from someone sitting a subject for the first time — more on that below.

## Step 1: Map Your Exact Exam Dates First

Before building any timetable, get your actual entry/timetable confirmation from your school's exams officer (or from the British Council if you registered privately). Write down, for every subject:

- The date of each paper (Paper 1, Paper 2, Paper 3/4, etc.)
- The gap between your papers — a three-day gap between two heavy subjects needs a very different plan than a two-week gap

Only once you have real dates should you start allocating study time. Working backward from a real deadline is what makes a revision timetable useful instead of aspirational.

## Step 2: Build Your Weekly Structure

**If you have 6–8 weeks left**, split the time roughly into three phases:

- **Weeks 1–3 — Rebuild and consolidate.** Go through the full syllabus for each subject, topic by topic, using your notes and a revision guide. Flag anything you genuinely don't understand rather than skimming past it.
- **Weeks 4–6 — Past papers and application.** This is where grades actually move. Start timed past-paper practice for every subject, marked against the official scheme, not just checked against a model answer.
- **Final 1–2 weeks — Targeted revision only.** No new topics. Revisit your weakest areas, redo papers you scored poorly on, and do a final pass of formulas, definitions and command words.

**If you have 3–4 weeks left**, compress the phases: roughly 40% consolidation, 50% past papers, 10% final review. Be honest about which topics have the biggest mark share and prioritise those first — see Step 3.

**If you're retaking a specific paper**, skip broad consolidation almost entirely. Go straight to past papers and examiner reports for that paper, focusing on exactly why marks were lost last time.

## Step 3: Prioritise by Marks, Not by Comfort

It's tempting to spend the most time on the topic you find most interesting, or avoid the one you find hardest. Neither is the right filter. Instead, rank topics by:

1. **Weighting in the exam** — a topic worth 20% of a paper deserves more hours than one worth 5%, regardless of how it feels.
2. **How often it appears in past papers** — some topics come up almost every session; others rarely.
3. **How far you are from a pass mark on it right now** — a weak topic worth a lot of marks is your highest-value use of time.

This applies whether you're revising Cambridge O Level Biology, IGCSE Mathematics, or A Level Chemistry — subject-specific past papers and examiner reports will tell you exactly where the marks are concentrated. If you're unsure where to start for a particular subject, your subject's exam board page is a good place to check the syllabus breakdown and paper structure — for example, [A Level Chemistry (Cambridge)](/a-levels/cambridge/chemistry/) or [IGCSE Mathematics (Cambridge)](/igcse/cambridge/mathematics/) both list the syllabus code, papers and topic list you'll need to plan around.

## Step 4: Make Past Papers Your Main Tool, Not a Last-Minute Add-On

Reading notes feels productive but rarely translates into exam marks. Timed past-paper practice, marked properly, is what closes the gap between "I understand this" and "I can score full marks on this under exam conditions." We've written a full system for this — see [How to Use Past Papers to Raise Your Grade in 2026](/blog/how-to-use-past-papers-effectively/) — but the short version for your revision timetable is: attempt a paper timed, mark it against the official scheme, and log every mark lost with a one-line reason before moving to the next paper.

## Step 5: Don't Forget the Practical / Alternative-to-Practical Papers

For science subjects, many students revise the theory papers thoroughly and forget that Paper 3 (Practical) or Paper 4/6 (Alternative to Practical) carries a real share of the total mark — often 20% or more. These papers reward specific, learnable skills: reading instruments correctly, describing experimental procedures precisely, and interpreting data or graphs. Build at least one dedicated practice session per subject into your timetable for this paper alone, especially in the final two weeks.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid in These Final Weeks

- **Studying every subject equally**, regardless of how many papers or marks are at stake in each.
- **Re-reading instead of practising** — recognising an answer is not the same as producing one under exam conditions.
- **Leaving past papers until the final week**, when there's no time left to fix the patterns they reveal.
- **Ignoring sleep and pacing** in favour of long, unfocused study sessions — tired revision produces weak recall on exam day.
