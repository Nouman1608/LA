Almost every Cambridge, Edexcel and AQA student has heard the same advice: "do past papers." Most do — and a lot of them barely move their grade doing it. The difference isn't how many papers you complete. It's whether you're using them as an active diagnostic tool or as a way to feel busy without addressing what's actually costing you marks.

Whether you're preparing for an October/November 2026 retake after this month's results, starting your first IGCSE or A Level exam series, or simply want a study system that works, here's how to use past papers properly.

## Why Past Papers Beat Re-Reading Notes

Re-reading a textbook chapter feels productive, but it mostly tests whether you recognise information — not whether you can retrieve and apply it under exam conditions, against a real mark scheme, in a fixed amount of time. Past papers force active recall and exam technique at the same time, which is exactly what's being tested on the day. This is why a student who "knows the content" can still underperform: knowing a topic and producing a mark-scheme-matching answer in eight minutes are different skills, and only one of them is trained by re-reading.

## Where to Get Genuine Past Papers

Use official sources wherever possible: your school or tutor's resource library, the Cambridge International School Support Hub, Pearson Edexcel's qualifications website, and AQA/OxfordAQA's website all publish genuine past papers and mark schemes for registered centres and candidates. If you're a private candidate without direct portal access, your tutor or exam centre — including the British Council — can usually provide papers for your specific syllabus code, which is worth confirming since syllabus codes and paper formats do change between years.

## The Right Way to Practise: Timed, Closed Book, Honest

The single biggest mistake is treating past papers as an open-book, untimed exercise. To get real value:

- **Set a timer** for the actual exam duration — no pausing, no notes, no phone.
- **Attempt every question**, even ones you're unsure about — exams don't let you skip, and partial marks matter.
- **Mark it yourself against the official mark scheme immediately after**, while the paper is still fresh.

Doing a paper "eventually, when there's time to check it properly" almost never happens. Mark it the same day.

## Mark Schemes Are Where the Marks Actually Are

Mark schemes aren't just an answer key — they show exactly what examiners are rewarding, point by point. Two things deserve close attention.

### Command words

"State," "explain," "compare," "evaluate" and "discuss" all require different types of answers. A "state" question wants a fact; an "explain" question wants a mechanism or reason; an "evaluate" question wants a judgement backed by both sides of an argument. Losing marks because you wrote a "state"-level answer to an "explain" question is one of the most common — and most fixable — patterns across Cambridge, Edexcel and AQA papers.

### Honest self-marking

Only award yourself a mark where your answer genuinely matches the marking point — not where it's "close enough." Generous self-marking hides exactly the gaps you're trying to find. If you're unsure whether an answer deserves the mark, that uncertainty is itself useful information about a topic you haven't fully nailed down.

## The Review Loop That Actually Moves Your Grade

Scoring a paper and moving straight to the next one wastes most of the value. The loop that works is: attempt, mark, diagnose, fix, retest.

After marking, go through every dropped mark and categorise why: a knowledge gap, a misread question, weak exam technique, or running out of time. These need different fixes. A knowledge gap needs revision. A misread question needs slower, more careful reading next time. Running out of time needs pacing practice, not more content review.

This plays out differently by subject. In [A Level Mathematics](/a-levels/cambridge/mathematics/), dropped marks usually cluster around a handful of question types — once you spot the pattern, targeted practice on just those types closes the gap fast. In sciences like [IGCSE Chemistry](/igcse/cambridge/chemistry/), marks are often lost on precise definitions and command-word answers rather than a lack of understanding. In essay-based subjects like [A Level Economics](/a-levels/edexcel/economics/), the gap is frequently in evaluation paragraphs — making a judgement and justifying it — rather than knowledge of the theory itself.

## How Many Papers Is Enough?

There's no universal number, but more papers without proper review is close to worthless. Three to five full papers per subject, thoroughly marked and reviewed, will move your grade further than ten papers rushed through without analysis. Quality of review, not quantity of papers, is the variable that matters.

## Building a Past-Paper Timetable Before Your Exam

If you're working toward the October/November 2026 series, you likely have a window of roughly eight to twelve weeks from results day. A workable structure:

- **Weeks 1–3: Diagnose.** Use one past paper per subject to identify your specific weak topics and question types, without worrying yet about full-paper timing.
- **Weeks 4–8: Fix and drill.** Target the specific gaps you found, then retest with a fresh past paper under timed conditions every one to two weeks.
- **Final 2 weeks: Full timed papers only**, mimicking real exam-day conditions as closely as possible, with mark-scheme review straight after each one.
