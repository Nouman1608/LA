Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry (4CH1) has one feature that shapes everything about how it should be taught, and most families only discover it late: there is no practical exam at all.

Cambridge students sit either a laboratory test or a written Alternative to Practical. Edexcel students sit neither. The whole qualification is two written papers, and experimental skills are examined *inside* those papers — a question will hand your child a method and a table of results and ask them to find the flaw, plot the data, or explain the anomaly. Nothing is assessed by a teacher, nothing needs a school laboratory, and nothing is banked as coursework.

For a Pakistani family that suits two situations especially well. If your child is a private candidate, or at a school whose laboratory provision is thin, 4CH1 removes the problem entirely. And because Edexcel runs both a January and a June series, a student who wants to sit early, retake, or split their subjects across two sittings has options the single-sitting route does not offer.

**What the two papers actually ask.** Paper 1 is the longer of the two — 110 marks over two hours, carrying 61.1% of the grade — and it draws on the core content of the specification. Paper 2 adds the more demanding material that Edexcel flags in the specification itself, in 70 marks over an hour and a quarter.

What the split does *not* mean is that Paper 2 is a separate syllabus. Both papers can pull from any of the four content areas — principles of chemistry, inorganic, physical and organic — so a student who has quietly written off a topic because "it came up in Paper 1 already" is exposed. We teach the specification as one body of knowledge and let the papers fall where they fall.

Edexcel also has a distinct examining habit: it likes to place familiar chemistry in an unfamiliar setting. A rates question may arrive dressed as an industrial process your child has never read about; the chemistry underneath is the chemistry they already know. Students who have only memorised worked examples freeze at this. Students who have been made to explain *why* a rate rises freeze far less, and that is a large part of what our lessons are for.

**Where the marks are quietly lost.** Three places, in our experience. Calculation questions where the working is right but the answer is given to the wrong number of significant figures or without units. Six-mark extended answers that list facts instead of building an argument. And the practical-analysis questions — the ones testing whether a student can read a method critically rather than perform one. All three are trainable, and none of them are about being clever.
